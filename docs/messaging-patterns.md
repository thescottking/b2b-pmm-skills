# Messaging Patterns

Eight patterns that do most of the work in B2B positioning copy. They are
structural, not stylistic, so they survive translation into any category. Pick
one per asset. Two stacked in the same post is one too many.

`content-writer`, `social-generator`, and `house-style` all read this file.
`content-writer` picks a pattern per asset, `social-generator` picks one per
caption, and `house-style` checks that at least one is present and scores the
draft on it. None of the three restates a pattern, so this file is the only
place to change one. The content each pattern operates on comes from
`brand-kit/positioning.md`: the frame, the pillars, the named traps, and the
vocabulary.

Examples below use Ampfield, the fictional field operations platform documented
in `examples/ampfield/`. Substitute your own.

---

## Start with the name

Of the eight, the Naming pattern is the one worth building a year of messaging
around. Everything else here is a way of delivering an argument. Naming changes
what the argument is about.

An unnamed problem is a complaint. Buyers experience it, work around it, and
have no way to raise it in a meeting, because describing it takes four sentences
and by sentence two it sounds like a personal grievance. A named problem is a
thing that exists. It can be put on a slide, brought to a budget conversation,
and repeated by someone who has never spoken to you.

Naming also moves the competitive fight onto ground you chose. A competitor can
answer a feature comparison. Answering a named trap requires them to either
adopt your language, which concedes the frame, or ignore it, which leaves it
standing. That is why the Trap pattern below is built on top of Naming rather
than beside it.

The cost is discipline. A name only becomes currency through repetition, which
means using the same words for eighteen months while you are personally bored of
them. Most companies name four things and abandon them at month three, which is
the same as never having named anything.

## 1. Sequence

**What it is.** State the mechanism as an ordered list of verbs. Four steps, one
word or one short phrase each.

> Capture. Reconcile. Flag. Close.

**When it works.** When the buyer's objection is "how does this actually work,"
which is most technical and operational audiences. It also works as a spine for
a demo, a landing page section, or the middle of a long post. A sequence makes a
product feel like a mechanism rather than a promise, and mechanism is what
earns trust from people who have been sold to before. A reader who can repeat
your sequence can explain you to their boss.

**When it backfires.** When the steps are generic. "Connect, analyze, optimize,
scale" describes every software product ever built and signals that you either
cannot explain your mechanism or do not have one. It also backfires when the
sequence is not actually sequential: if step three can happen before step two,
the reader notices, and the whole structure reads as decoration.

**Worked example.**

> The technician taps done on the roof. The record reaches the office in about
> 90 seconds. Truck stock decrements against what actually came off the shelf.
> Dispatch sees the same job state the warranty desk sees.

Each step is a real event with a real actor. Contrast that with "capture,
integrate, synchronize, optimize," which contains no information.

## 2. Correction

**What it is.** Take a belief the market holds and reverse it in one line, then
spend the rest of the piece earning the reversal.

> You do want your technicians documenting more, not less.

**When it works.** When the market's default belief is genuinely wrong and you
can prove it. It is the strongest opening line available for an audience that
thinks it already understands your category, because it interrupts a reader who
was about to skim.

**When it backfires.** Two ways. First, when the belief you are correcting is a
strawman nobody actually holds, which makes the whole piece feel like shadow
boxing. Second, when you cannot deliver the proof, in which case you have
spent the reader's attention on a claim and then handed them nothing. The
correction has to be followed immediately by the reason, not three paragraphs
later.

**Worked example.**

> Every field software pitch starts by promising less paperwork for technicians.
> Ampfield asks them to record more than the clipboard did. The difference is
> where it goes: a serial number scanned on the roof is two taps and it prevents
> a denied warranty claim eight months later. A form filled out at 6pm is
> fifteen minutes and it prevents nothing.

The correction is stated flatly, and the next two sentences pay for it.

## 3. Provoke

**What it is.** A short, declarative sentence that indicts the reader's
organization rather than their vendor.

> Your dispatch board is a plan, not a picture.

**When it works.** As a hook for cold audiences on social, or as the first line
of an email to someone who does not know you. It works because it is about them,
not you, and because it is uncomfortable enough to be read twice. Pair it with a
pattern that resolves the discomfort. Provoke opens; it does not close. One per
asset, near the top.

**When it backfires.** When it insults the reader's competence rather than
naming a systemic problem. "Your operations team is disorganized" gets you
blocked. "Nobody is trying to hide anything, the information just had nowhere to
go for four hours" gets you read, because it removes blame from the people while
keeping the problem intact. It also backfires when overused: a feed of nothing
but provocations reads as a personality, not a company.

**Worked example.**

> Your company does not have a scheduling problem. It has a 6pm problem. Every
> decision made between 2:40 and 6:15 is made against a version of the day that
> already changed.

The indictment lands on the process. The reader is a witness, not the defendant.

## 4. Trap

**What it is.** The most complete pattern here, and the spine of most positioning
work. Name the trap, then run three beats: the limitation, the failure it
causes, and your difference.

Structure:

1. **Name the trap.** Title case, definite article, defined in one sentence on
   first use.
2. **Limitation.** What the common approach does, described fairly.
3. **Failure.** What breaks because of it, described concretely.
4. **Difference.** How you resolve it, in one line, with no adjectives.

**When it works.** Anywhere the reader has lived the problem and never had a
word for it. It is the strongest structure for a pillar article, a sales
conversation opener, and a battlecard. It also compounds: once four or five
traps are named and in circulation, new content can reference one in a clause
instead of rebuilding the argument.

**When it backfires.** When the trap is really a feature gap wearing a costume.
Buyers can tell the difference between a name for a problem they have and a name
for a thing your competitor lacks. It also backfires when you name too many:
past eight or so, none of them stick, and the vocabulary starts to feel like a
private language rather than a shared one. `Named Traps` in
`brand-kit/positioning.md` caps at four to eight for this reason.

**Worked example.**

> **The Second Truck Roll.** A crew arrives, diagnoses, and cannot finish:
> wrong part, missing spec, no photo of the panel from the last visit. Someone
> books a return.
>
> **The limitation.** Dispatch assigns work against a parts list that was
> accurate at the depot last Tuesday.
>
> **The failure.** The return is priced internally as a new job and experienced
> by the customer as a failure. It consumes a revenue slot, and the cause is
> almost never recorded, so it repeats next week.
>
> **The difference.** Truck Stock Ledger tells dispatch what is on each truck
> before the job is assigned, and the previous visit's record travels with the
> job.

## 5. Reframe

**What it is.** Take a stated objection and expose the real concern underneath
it.

> "We already have a mobile app." What they mean: "I do not want to run another
> adoption fight with my technicians."

**When it works.** In sales enablement, objection-handling content, and any
piece written for a reader who has already said no once. It works because it
demonstrates that you have heard the objection enough times to know what it
covers for, which is a credential no amount of product copy buys. Agree with the
diagnosis, push back on the prescription.

**When it backfires.** When the reframe is condescending, or when it is wrong.
Telling a buyer that their stated concern is not their real concern is a strong
move, and it collapses if the underlying concern you name is not one they
recognize. Test it on three real customers before it goes in a deck. And never
use the pattern to dismiss a legitimate objection: if the answer to "we already
have a mobile app" is that yours is better, say that instead.

**Worked example.**

> "Our technicians will never adopt it." What they actually mean is that the
> last rollout added taps to a job that was already long, and the field found a
> way around it within a month. That is not a resistance problem. It is a design
> constraint, and it belongs in the evaluation: measure taps per job during the
> pilot and hold the vendor to the number.

## 6. Naming

**What it is.** Give a problem a memorable label. Once it has a name, the reader
recognizes it in their own organization and can describe it to a colleague
without you in the room.

**When it works.** When the problem is real, common, currently unnamed, and
expensive. All four conditions matter. A named problem that is not expensive
becomes a joke rather than a budget line.

Good names are concrete and slightly physical. Two-word noun phrases travel
furthest. The Clipboard Gap works because there is a clipboard in it. The
Rebuild Tax works because "tax" implies a recurring cost nobody chose. Abstract
names built from category jargon do not travel.

Every name you keep belongs in `Named Traps` in `brand-kit/positioning.md` so it
stays consistent across every asset.

**When it backfires.** When the name describes your product instead of their
problem. A name with your company in it is a feature, not a frame, and buyers
will not repeat it. It also backfires when you name something that already has a
widely used name, which reads as an attempt to own a word rather than a problem.

**Worked example.**

> **The Rebuild Tax.** The first hours of every morning go to reconstructing
> yesterday: chasing photos, correcting counts, closing jobs the field already
> considers finished. It appears on no report, because it is nobody's job title.
> It is simply what operations does before it starts working, and it scales with
> headcount, so growth makes it worse.

Two sentences of definition, one sentence of why it is invisible, one sentence of
why it gets worse. That is the whole shape.

## 7. Specificity

**What it is.** Replace every adjective with a number, a duration, or a count.

- Not "fast." Say "within about 90 seconds of the technician tapping done."
- Not "expensive." Say "one revenue slot per return visit."
- Not "many systems." Say "six systems to close one job."
- Not "slow to close." Say "the record arrives at 6:15pm for a job that
  finished at 2:40."
- Not "long." Give the cycle length in days.
- Not "a lot of duplicated work." Give the percentage.

**When it works.** Always, in body copy. It is less a pattern than a discipline,
and it is the single fastest upgrade available to a weak draft. `Clarity` in
`brand-kit/voice.md` states the rule; this is the applied version.

**When it backfires.** When the number is invented. A specific number is more
persuasive than an adjective and correspondingly more damaging when a buyer
checks it and it is not real. Every number in customer-facing copy comes from
`Headline Stats` in `brand-kit/positioning.md`, with a source, and anything
carrying an unverified flag does not ship.

It also backfires when the number is precise but meaningless. "Improves
efficiency by 34%" answers nothing without a baseline, a measurement window, and
what was measured.

**Worked example.**

> Weak: our platform dramatically reduces costly repeat visits.
>
> Specific: 31% of second visits happen because the first crew did not have the
> part. Dispatch could have known that before it assigned the job.

The second version can be argued with, which is what makes it worth reading.

## 8. Sales Question

**What it is.** End a piece with a question the reader can take into a vendor
call, including yours.

> Ask any vendor in this category: when a technician finishes a job at 2:40,
> what time does the office have a usable record of it, and what happens if the
> site had no signal?

**When it works.** As a closer for competitive content, comparison pages, and
posts aimed at buyers in an active evaluation. It works because it changes the
reader's role. They stop being sold to and start being the person running the
test, and the test is one you are confident about.

**When it backfires.** When the question is transparently rigged, which readers
detect instantly. If only your product can answer it, it is a feature list with a
question mark. The question has to be one a reasonable buyer would think of on
their own, that a competent competitor could attempt, and that you simply answer
better.

It also backfires as a general-purpose closer. Used on every asset, it becomes a
tic. One per asset, at the close, and only on the pieces where the reader is
genuinely comparing.

**Worked example.**

> Before you sign anything in this category, ask for one number: on a job that
> finished in an area with no cell coverage, how long until the office can see
> what happened, and can the crew keep working while it is offline? Every vendor
> has an answer. The answers are not the same.
